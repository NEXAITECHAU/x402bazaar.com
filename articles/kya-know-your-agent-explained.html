Know Your Agent (KYA): The Identity Standard for Autonomous AI Transactions (2026)

By NEX Agent Co. · 10 min read · Updated September 2026

By the end of 2026, autonomous AI agents will execute more than 217 billion actions per day and consume 3.7 trillion inference tokens daily (IDC). Every one of those actions involves an agent making a decision on behalf of a human or an organization. Every one of those actions is a potential liability, fraud vector, or compliance violation — unless someone knows who the agent is, who authorized it, and what it's allowed to do.

That question is what Know Your Agent (KYA) exists to answer. It's the missing trust layer for AI commerce, and the standards are landing right now.

What KYA is, in one sentence

KYA is a set of cryptographic and reputational standards that lets a merchant, payment processor, or API gateway verify, before a transaction settles, four specific things about the AI agent making the request:

  1. **Who is this agent?** — A signed identity credential, not just an API key.
  2. **Who authorized it?** — A verifiable delegation chain from a human or organization.
  3. **What is it allowed to do?** — Declared spending limits, allowed counterparties, prohibited actions.
  4. **Has it behaved consistently?** — Transaction history vs. declared intent, with anomaly detection.

    5. A payment that can't answer all four is, by KYA standards, unauthorized — regardless of whether the credentials themselves are technically valid.

    Why this matters in 2026

    Three converging forces are pushing KYA from "interesting" to "table stakes":

    1. Agent commerce is real and growing

    McKinsey projects the global agentic commerce market will reach $3-5 trillion by 2030. Gartner projects 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from less than 5% twelve months earlier. The market is no longer a thought experiment — money is moving through agents today.

    2. Fraud is following the money

    Card-not-present fraud is the largest category of payment fraud globally. AI agents transacting at scale will amplify the problem if every agent presents a stolen card indistinguishably from a legitimate one. KYA gives the payment network a real-time signal: *this card present came from a verified agent operating under user X's verified authorization*.

    3. Regulation is catching up

    The EU AI Act enforcement mechanisms began August 2026. Combined with regulatory analysis from Taylor Wessing (Feb 2026), the writing is on the wall: agent identity and transaction log requirements will be formalized within 18 months. Building KYA-compliant infrastructure now is lower cost than retrofitting it under a deadline.

    The 5 KYA standards that mattered in 2026

    Five major KYA standards have shipped or reached production in 2026. They're not directly compatible with each other, but they all implement the same four-pillar model.

    1. Visa Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP)

    The largest by partner footprint. Launched October 14, 2025 with twelve initial partners on the merchant and processor side: Adyen, Ant International, Checkout.com, Coinbase, CyberSource, Elavon, Fiserv, Microsoft, Nuvei, Shopify, Stripe, Worldpay.

    TAP attaches three signed HTTP headers to every agent request — Signature-Agent, Signature-Input, and Signature — using RFC 9421 HTTP Message Signatures built on the IETF Web Bot Auth draft. Merchants verify the Ed25519 signature against a Visa-operated directory.

    Status as of September 2026: Completing live transactions in production pilots, with 21 issuing partners in the European "Agentic Ready" program (Barclays, HSBC UK, Revolut, Commerzbank, Nationwide, Raiffeisen Bank International, Santander).

    2. Skyfire KYAPay

    Uses signed JWTs to attach agent identity to HTTP requests. The JWT names the agent operator and the user delegation. Independent infrastructure — can be deployed without Visa.

    Status: Production, growing adoption among agentic payment startups and SaaS platforms that want KYA without the Visa network dependency.

    3. Mastercard Agentic Tokens (Agent Pay)

    Structurally different from the others: instead of layering identity on top of HTTP, it embeds agent identity into the network token used for the card transaction itself. The Agentic Token carries an agent-identity claim alongside the existing tokenized PAN, so the issuer can see at authorization time which agent operator made the request and apply network-level rules.

    Unveiled April 29, 2025; full US rollout November 2025. The "Verifiable Intent" primitive is Mastercard's term for KYA's capability-boundaries pillar.

    4. Google AP2 (Agent Payments Protocol)

    Cryptographically signs the user's authorization for the agent to spend, not the agent's identity to the merchant. AP2 launched September 2025 with 60+ partners including American Express, Coinbase, Mastercard, and PayPal. Spec is open-source on GitHub.

    Key insight: AP2 solves a different problem than TAP or Agent Pay. It answers "did the human delegate this purchase to this agent?" rather than "is the agent registered?". The two are complementary.

    5. Akamai Know Your Agent (KYA) Framework

    Launched June 15, 2026 with Visa, Experian, and Skyfire as launch partners. The first major attempt to build a financial-grade identity layer for AI agents transacting on behalf of humans, with edge enforcement on Akamai's CDN.

    The framework includes six pillars: verified agent identity (KYA), adaptive trust analysis (behavioral scoring), runtime threat detection, secure communication channels, audit trail, and policy enforcement. Auth0 and Ping Identity are identity provider integrations.

    Sits between: Vercel Passport (intra-enterprise governance) and Cloudflare AI Identity (runtime workload identity). KYA is the *cross-enterprise transaction* layer.

    Bonus: ERC-8004 (blockchain-native KYA)

    Created August 2025, deployed to Ethereum mainnet January 29, 2026. Co-authored by engineers from MetaMask, the Ethereum Foundation, Google, and Coinbase.

    Defines three lightweight singleton registries:

    • **Identity Registry (ERC-721)** — Each agent receives a unique `agentId` (token ID) and an `agentURI` pointing to JSON metadata with service endpoints, trust configuration, and protocol support (Google A2A, Anthropic MCP, ENS, W3C DIDs).
    • **Reputation Registry** — Standardized interface for posting signed feedback signals (quality ratings, uptime, response times). Core data on-chain; detailed evidence via IPFS. Explicitly prevents self-review.
    • **Validation Registry** — Agents can request verification from independent validator smart contracts. Supports TEE attestations, zero-knowledge proofs, and stake-secured re-execution without prescribing methodology.

    Why it matters: ERC-8004 is the only blockchain-native KYA standard. It works without any fiat intermediary. It composes with existing crypto infrastructure (USDC, x402, on-chain reputation systems). NEX Agent Co. is the first x402 service to issue ERC-8004-style signed agent passports.

    The four pillars of any KYA implementation

    Whether you're building on Visa TAP, Skyfire KYAPay, Mastercard Agent Pay, or your own in-house system, you need to implement four specific checks. A payment that can't answer all four is unauthorized.

    Pillar 1: Agent Identity

    A cryptographically signed credential that identifies this specific agent instance: which model, which version, which deployment, issued by whom. The parallel to a passport is intentional.

    NEX implementation: /v1/agent-passport issues a signed JSON credential with kya_version: erc-8004-v1, agent_id, operator, scope, jurisdiction, and an HMAC-SHA256 signature. $0.01 per issuance.

    Pillar 2: Authorization Chain

    A verifiable record of delegation: the human or organization that controls the agent, the scope of authority granted, and whether that authority extends to the transaction being requested. A procurement agent authorized to buy software licenses should not be able to approve payroll.

    Pillar 3: Capability Boundaries

    Declared and enforced limits on what the agent can do: spending ceilings per transaction, per day, or per category; allowed counterparties; prohibited action types. Boundaries declared at registration and enforced at the payment layer, not inside agent code — because agent code can be modified or jailbroken.

    Pillar 4: Behavioral Reputation

    Consistency of prior actions against declared intent. An agent that has never exceeded a $200 transaction limit suddenly requesting $5,000 warrants a hold, regardless of valid credentials. This is where the reputation registries come in — both Visa's internal scoring and ERC-8004's on-chain reputation registry.

    The transaction-time checklist

    At the moment a transaction request arrives, a KYA-compliant payment processor runs these four checks in under 100ms:

    
1. Verify the agent credential is current and has not been revoked
2. Confirm the requested transaction falls within declared capability boundaries
3. Check behavioral consistency: is this transaction anomalous vs. prior history?
4. Log the full authorization chain — agent ID, delegation record,
   transaction amount, merchant, timestamp — before the payment clears

    Step 4 is non-negotiable. Without the audit trail, you have no defense when something goes wrong at 2 AM.

    What KYA doesn't solve

    KYA is a verification layer, not a governance framework. Three things remain on the operator's side:

    1. **What the agent is allowed to do in its own code.** KYA verifies the declaration; it doesn't enforce it inside the agent.
    2. **What the agent is allowed to see in its training data.** KYA governs transactions, not data access.
    3. **What the agent does offline.** If the agent has been jailbroken and is acting outside its declared scope, KYA's behavioral reputation scoring will catch it eventually — but the offline behavior is on the operator.

      4. The right mental model: KYA is the security guard at the door. It checks IDs and decides who gets in. It doesn't watch what happens inside.

      How to get started with KYA

      For a team deploying AI agents that transact:

      1. **Register your agent** with a KYA provider. Visa TAP for fiat-first, ERC-8004 for crypto-native, Akamai for edge enforcement, Skyfire for HTTP-native. NEX's `/v1/agent-passport` works for crypto-native x402 transactions.
      2. **Declare capability boundaries.** Spending limits, allowed counterparties, prohibited action types. Be specific. "Up to $5,000 per day in software licenses from approved vendors" is a good declaration. "Reasonable purchases" is not.
      3. **Log every transaction** with the full authorization chain. This is your audit trail.
      4. **Monitor for behavioral anomalies** against declared intent. The reputation registries do this for you; check them weekly.
      5. **Plan for the EU AI Act.** August 2026 enforcement is the first major deadline. Your agent identity and transaction log requirements will need to be formalized within 18 months.

        6. For a payment processor or API gateway integrating KYA verification:

        1. **Pick a verification provider.** Visa for fiat cards, ERC-8004 for crypto, both for coverage.
        2. **Run the four checks in under 100ms.** This is the latency budget.
        3. **Store the authorization chain** — agent ID, delegation record, transaction amount, merchant, timestamp. Keep it for at least 7 years.
        4. **Surface KYA status to the merchant.** "Verified agent acting under user X with $200/day limit" is a much better signal than "this transaction was approved".
        5. **Build anomaly detection** on top of behavioral reputation data. The first 30 days of an agent's life are the highest-risk period for fraud.
